How the hackathon judges score a project

A plain-English guide to the 10 judging criteria, with examples, applied to PlantPay. Written for the team (Sanket, Lokesh, Utkarsh) so everyone can explain the scoring and answer judges' questions. Dated 13 Nov 2026.

How to use this. Each chapter answers five questions: (1) what the criterion means in simple words, (2) what judges look for, (3) a strong example against a weak example, (4) how PlantPay shows it and where to click, (5) what to say on stage. The last chapter explains how our estimated score of about 86.5 / 100 was worked out. Scores are our own estimates, not measured results.

Our project in one paragraph (so every example below makes sense)

PlantPay Intelligence Engine is a Procure-to-Pay assistant for a fictional plant, Plant X. A supplier uploads an invoice. PlantPay reads six fields, then checks it against the purchase order (PO) and the goods receipt note (GRN) pulled from six separate department databases (Procurement, Warehouse, Quality, Finance, Logistics, Supplier). Fixed rules decide approve, flag or block. If something does not match, PlantPay says why, who owns the fix and what exact change would make it pass. Approvals are routed by amount, every step goes into a tamper-evident audit trail, and a supplier can ask "Where is my payment?". The key demo case is INV-7781: invoice ₹412 per kg against a PO of ₹398.

1. Problem understanding & user fit 10 points · our estimate 9

What it means

Judges want proof that you understand the real pain of the people who will use your product, not a generic "AI for finance" story. In this problem the users are three people: the supplier, the AP clerk (accounts payable) and the approver. Each has a different pain, so a single generic dashboard shows you did not think about them separately.

What judges look for

  • The pain stated in everyday words: large invoice volumes, mismatches, duplicates, missing approvals, late payments, audit risk.
  • Different screens for different roles, each speaking to that role's own frustration.
  • The problem visible inside the app, not only in the slide deck.

The three users and their pain

UserTheir pain, in their wordsWhat PlantPay gives them
Supplier (e.g. Vihar Chem Supplies)"I sent the invoice three weeks ago. I don't know where my payment is, and nobody replies to my emails."A payment readiness passport (what is holding payment, who owns it, when it is due) and a chat that answers "Where is my payment?" from live records.
AP clerk (e.g. Priya Nair)"I spend hours chasing mismatches: ringing the buyer, the store, the supplier, just to find out why a price is different."Every exception arrives with a root cause, an owner and a suggested fix. The queue is sorted oldest first.
Approver (e.g. Neha Kulkarni, Finance Manager)"I have no idea what needs my sign-off, and when I do get something I can't see the evidence."An inbox of only the items for their approval level, already matched clean, with the evidence on one screen.

Strong example versus weak example

Strong: "Plant X receives hundreds of invoices a month. A clerk checks each one by hand against the PO and goods receipt. When the price on the invoice is ₹412 but the PO says ₹398, the clerk spends days emailing the buyer to find out the contract was changed and nobody updated the PO. Meanwhile the supplier is waiting and the auditor will later ask who approved what."
Weak: "Finance teams face inefficiencies in invoice processing. Our AI platform leverages machine learning to optimise the procure-to-pay lifecycle."
This could describe any product at any company. A judge learns nothing about Plant X.

How PlantPay shows it

  • Login page: one paragraph naming Plant X and the problems (mismatches, duplicates, missing approvals).
  • "Why we built this" banner: on the clerk home page and the PlantPay home page.
  • Role banners: each role sees a welcome message with live numbers. Clerk: invoices matched clean, hours saved, exceptions that arrived with an owner. Approver: items waiting, their value, the oldest age. Supplier: open invoices, how many are cleared and how many have a named owner working on them.
  • Separate role views: three logins with different menus. A supplier cannot see the staff pages.

What to say on stage

"Three people feel this problem differently. The supplier asks 'where is my money'. The clerk asks 'why doesn't this match'. The approver asks 'what do I need to sign'. We built one screen for each of them, then we built the engine underneath."

Why 9 and not 10

The supplier and approver messages use measured counts, but the "payment wait reduced from X to Y days" comparison depends on an assumed baseline. Without real Plant X data we cannot prove the "before" number.

2. Innovation & original thinking 10 points · our estimate 9

What it means

Almost every team in a procure-to-pay hackathon will build three-way matching: compare invoice, PO and goods receipt, and say "mismatch". Judges will have seen that many times. Innovation here means going beyond detecting the problem to explaining it and fixing it, in a way a standard system does not.

What judges look for

  • Something they have not seen in the other presentations.
  • The idea being useful, not just clever. Novelty for its own sake does not score.
  • The features being visibly working, not only described on a slide.

Strong example versus weak example

Standard (what everyone builds): "Invoice INV-7781 does not match PO 4600009003. Status: mismatch."
The clerk still has to find out why.
Innovative (what PlantPay does): "The invoice price is ₹412. The PO says ₹398. The contract was amended on 1 October to ₹412, but the PO was never updated. Owner: Buyer. This invoice would pass if the PO price were updated to ₹412." Evidence from Procurement is shown, and a sandbox button simulates the fix.

PlantPay's innovations, in plain words

Root-Cause ResolverNames why an invoice fails, who owns the fix, and shows evidence. A cause is marked "evidence-supported" only when a record backs it; otherwise it says "unverified prediction"./plantpay/invoices/INV-7781/exceptions
Counterfactual explanationThe smallest change that would make the invoice pass ("what would make this pass"), plus a sandbox "simulate" button.Same page
Process-mining twinReplays every workflow event to show the path an invoice actually took versus the expected path, where time goes, and where work loops back./plantpay/process

What to say on stage

"Every system flags a mismatch. None of them tells you the contract was amended, the PO wasn't updated, the buyer owns it, and that changing the PO to ₹412 would make this pass. That last step is what we built."

Why 9 and not 10

The ideas are strong, but some views (the graph, the process twin) are not drag-interactive. Judges who like polish may mark that down slightly.

3. Working prototype / functionality 20 points · our estimate 18

What it means

This is the single biggest criterion: 20% of the entire score. It asks: does it really work, live, with no excuses? A prototype that is half-finished or crashes when a judge clicks something scores badly here no matter how good the idea is.

What judges look for

  • The core flow works end to end without manual patching: upload, extract, match, approve / flag / block.
  • It works on several different inputs, not just one rehearsed example.
  • No crashes, no raw error text, no blank screens.
  • Reasonable speed: pages that load in seconds, not minutes.

Strong example versus weak example

Strong: A judge asks "Can you try one with a missing goods receipt?" and the presenter uploads a different document; the system flags it with a clear reason within seconds.
Weak: The demo works only on one hard-wired invoice. When a judge asks to try another, the page shows an error or the presenter says "it only works on this one for now".

Evidence: our production smoke test ran 786 checks and all 786 passed, with zero crashes. It covers every page, all demo documents, unknown and malicious ids, simulated outages of each source, the whole supplier-clerk-approver workflow three times, and the Golden Journey three times.

What to say on stage

"Everything you are about to see is running live on the real build. If you would like to try a different invoice, pick any of the demo documents."

Why 18 and not 20

  • No human has yet clicked through the new pages in a real browser on the demo laptop.
  • The Predictions page takes about 2 to 5 seconds the first time after the server starts (the target was under 3). Open it once before judges arrive.
  • One unit test fails because it hard-codes the date 12-Nov while the real date is now 13-Nov. It does not affect the app, but it is a loose end.

4. Technical design & architecture 10 points · our estimate 8.5

What it means

Can you explain how it is built and why? Judges want a clean structure with sensible separation of parts, not a tangle that happens to work.

What judges look for

  • A clear architecture they can understand in a minute: a diagram helps.
  • Sensible choices: each part has one job.
  • Clear separation between rules (decisions) and AI/ML (predictions and explanations).
  • Documentation of the interfaces, such as an API reference.

The key architecture idea, simply

Plant X's data lives in six separate department databases. A real plant never has everything in one place. PlantPay puts a read-only integration layer in front of them:

  • Connectors, one per database, that only read.
  • A canonical data model: one common shape for a supplier, PO, goods receipt, inspection and invoice, whatever each system calls them.
  • An event stream: when a PO or receipt changes, an event is written and only the affected invoices are re-checked.
  • A REST facade: an API that needs a key or a signed-in session, and logs every request.

On top sits the intelligence engine: matching rules, risk detectors, explanations and predictions. On top of that sits the dashboard.

Rules versus AI: the separation that matters

JobDone byWhy
Approve / flag / block an invoiceDeterministic rulesSame answer every time; auditors can read the rule.
Predict delay, risk or exception likelihoodTrained modelsHelps people prioritise; never decides.
Explain a causeRules plus evidenceShown as fact only when a record backs it.
Approve paymentA humanAccountability stays with a person.

Strong example versus weak example

Strong: "If the Warehouse database is offline, only the receipt check is marked 'source unavailable'. Everything else continues. The architecture page shows why."
Weak: "We used AI to match the invoices." A judge asks which part is AI, and the answer is vague.

How PlantPay shows it

  • Architecture page: the diagram, three bullets on how data flows, a "how the integration layer works" panel, and live source health.
  • Labels: "Rule-based" or "AI-assisted. Human review required" on the main feature pages.
  • API reference page: every endpoint listed.

What to say on stage

"Rules decide, AI explains, people approve. Six systems, one integration layer, one common record. If one source goes down, the rest keeps working: I will show that now."

Why 8.5 and not 10

The API documentation is a static reference page, not an interactive Swagger-style explorer. Labels are on the main feature pages rather than on every single output.

5. Feasibility & scalability 10 points · our estimate 8

What it means

A hackathon demo runs on one laptop with a few hundred invoices. Judges ask: could this survive in the real plant, with thousands of invoices, many users and real systems?

What judges look for

  • An honest statement of what is a demo shortcut and what the production path is.
  • Graceful handling of failure: one system down must not kill everything.
  • Signs the team thought about performance, such as saving trained models rather than retraining each time.

Strong example versus weak example

Strong: "The demo uses SQLite so it runs on a laptop. The connectors sit behind an interface, so production swaps each one to PostgreSQL without touching the rules or screens. Re-matching is event-driven, so several workers can process the queue at the same time."
Weak: "It's scalable because we used the cloud." No specifics, nothing a judge can test.

The five production-readiness points on the Architecture page

  1. Database swap: SQLite to PostgreSQL by replacing the connector.
  2. Horizontal scaling: matching depends only on one invoice's record, so workers can run in parallel.
  3. Authentication: demo has signed sessions and API keys; production adds single sign-on and per-role scopes.
  4. Audit compliance: the hash chain records who, role, time, outcome and comment; production also stores the chain head in write-once storage.
  5. Connector extensibility: a new source is one connector plus a mapping to the canonical model.

Evidence it handles failure

The smoke test takes each of the six sources offline, one at a time, and checks every affected page. 76 of 76 outage checks pass. On the PlantPay home page you can click "Simulate outage" on any source live.

Caching and speed

Trained models are saved to disk (data/ml/models.json) and loaded once, not retrained at every start. Each invoice's evaluation is stored, so a repeat page load reads the saved result.

What to say on stage

"SQLite is a demo convenience, not a design choice. Watch what happens when I take Warehouse offline: the goods receipt check says 'source unavailable' and the rest carries on."

Why 8 and not 10

Scalability is argued by design, not proven by a load test. We did not run thousands of invoices through the system under concurrent users.

6. Business / user value & impact 15 points · our estimate 12

What it means

The second-biggest criterion. "It saves time" is not enough. Judges want numbers: how much money, how many hours, how many days, for whom.

What judges look for

  • At least three quantified impact metrics, visible in the app.
  • Value shown to each role separately.
  • A before / after comparison: what happens without the product.
  • Honesty about which numbers are measured and which are assumed.

Strong example versus weak example

Weak: "PlantPay will save finance teams a lot of time and reduce errors."
Strong: "In our demo data, PlantPay held back ₹X of possible duplicate payments, matched N invoices clean on the first pass (about Y clerk hours saved at 12 minutes each), and gave M of K exceptions a named root cause and owner at match time, instead of about three days of chasing."

These appear on the Exceptions dashboard and as an Impact summary in the Controls report, with a "What would happen without PlantPay?" table. Assumptions are tagged "assumption" on screen. All data is synthetic.

What to say on stage

"The rupee values and counts come straight from the demo records. The time savings use three assumptions that you can see on screen, and Plant X's finance team can replace them with their own numbers in a minute."

Why 12 and not 15

This is the largest gap. We have no real Plant X data, so part of the case rests on assumptions. A judge may discount the time savings. Being open about this scores better than pretending the numbers are real.

7. Responsible AI, security & data 10 points · our estimate 9

What it means

If software helps decide who gets paid, mistakes hurt real people. Judges want to see that AI outputs are explainable, labelled, auditable and safe, and that data is protected.

What judges look for

  • AI predictions that show the reasons behind them.
  • Clear labels separating AI output from rule output.
  • A human making the final decision.
  • An audit trail that cannot be quietly altered.
  • Access control: one supplier cannot see another's data.
  • Protected APIs.

Strong example versus weak example

Weak: "This vendor is fraudulent." An AI accusing a supplier on a hunch is dangerous: shared bank accounts can be completely legitimate, for example within a group of companies.
Strong: "Warning, not proof. Two suppliers share a bank account. Human review required." The system creates a review task. It never blocks payment on a risk flag alone.

PlantPay's commitments

CommitmentHow it is enforced
Rules decide, AI explainsModels are not in the decision path.
Predictions show reasonsContributing factors are listed; probabilities come from trained, evaluated models, not hard-coded.
Unverified means unverifiedA predicted cause is labelled "unverified prediction" unless a record supports it.
Warnings, not accusationsDuplicate, splitting, vendor and graph flags read "Warning, not proof. Human review required."
Bank changes need a second checkMarked "Requires independent verification".
Humans stay in the loopApproval limits by role; whoever worked on an invoice cannot approve it; rejections need a written reason.
Verifiable audit trailHash chain plus a "Verify integrity" button.
Data isolationA supplier sees only its own invoices and never internal AP notes.
API securityEvery call needs an API key or a clerk or approver session; otherwise 401.
Synthetic data onlyNo real LYB data or systems.

These are collected on the Responsible AI page.

A simple way to explain the hash chain

Imagine a notebook where each page begins with a fingerprint of the previous page. If someone rewrites page 10, the fingerprint on page 11 no longer matches, and so on down the book. "Verify integrity" re-checks all the fingerprints and shows where the chain breaks. We even demonstrate tampering with a copy in the Golden Journey.

What to say on stage

"Our AI is deliberately boxed in. It can predict and explain, but it cannot approve, and it cannot accuse. Every flag says 'warning, not proof'."

Why 9 and not 10

The Rule-based / AI-assisted labels are on the main feature pages but not on literally every output on every screen.

8. User experience & accessibility 5 points · our estimate 4

What it means

Does it look professional, and can a busy clerk use it without training? Accessibility means people with different abilities, such as colour-blind users, can use it too.

What judges look for

  • Consistent colours, cards, badges and fonts across all pages.
  • No raw JSON, error traces or default-grey unstyled pages.
  • Status colours that mean something: green pass, amber warning, red block.
  • Every page has a title and a one-sentence description.
  • Friendly empty states and loading indicators.

Strong example versus weak example

Weak: A blank white page with a table of raw database rows, or "undefined" in a cell.
Strong: "No invoices match these filters. Clear filters" with a link, in the same card style as the rest of the app.

What PlantPay does

  • One dark design system: shared colour tokens, 12px rounded cards, pill-shaped status badges.
  • Status chips carry a shape cue as well as a colour, so meaning does not depend on colour alone.
  • Indian number grouping and the ₹ sign; dates like 12-Nov-2026.
  • Tables checked at 900px wide with no sideways page scroll.
  • The smoke test fails the build if any page shows "undefined", "NaN", a stack trace or similar text.
  • Loading spinners on the dashboard and audit pages.

Why 4 and not 5

Spinners are not on every page. We tried that, but a loading screen on every route turned "not found" pages into HTTP 200, which weakened a security check (a supplier opening another supplier's invoice should get a 404). Skeleton loaders, side drawers and animated gauges were left out because of the code freeze.

9. Presentation & demo 5 points · our estimate 4.5

What it means

Ten minutes per team. Judges score how smooth, clear and rehearsed the demo feels. A great product with a shaky demo loses points.

What judges look for

  • A clear story, not a feature tour.
  • No awkward pauses, wrong clicks or "let me just fix this".
  • A plan for when something goes wrong.
  • Staying within time.

Strong example versus weak example

Weak: The presenter hunts through menus, a page fails to load, and they say "this worked earlier".
Strong: A live call fails, the app shows "Fallback mode. Pre-computed results are shown", and the presenter carries on with the same story without stopping.

What PlantPay provides

  • Golden Journey: nine guided steps with a stepper, Back and Next, and arrow keys.
  • Presenter notes per step with a timer (about one minute each, nine minutes in all).
  • Automatic fallback to pre-computed results if any live call fails, with a "try live data again" button. Force it with ?fallback=1.
  • Feature badges on screen when each feature appears.
  • Closing checklist showing every feature ticked.
  • DEMO.md: the exact click path, what to say, what to point at, and a fallback plan.

Tips for the 10 minutes

  • Start the story with the problem, not the technology.
  • Use one invoice (INV-7781) as the thread through everything.
  • Warm up the Predictions page and the Journey before you go on.
  • Decide who speaks and who clicks. Rehearse with a stopwatch.
  • Keep the Q&A sheet open on another screen.

Why 4.5 and not 5

The script has not been timed against a stopwatch on the venue laptop yet.

10. Q&A & team understanding 5 points · our estimate 4.5

What it means

After the demo, judges ask questions. They are checking that everyone on the team understands the system, not only the person who built it. The hackathon rules also say every member must be able to explain all the code.

What judges look for

  • Direct, confident answers, not waffle.
  • Honesty about limits ("that is an assumption", "we did not load-test that").
  • More than one team member able to answer.

Strong example versus weak example

Weak: Q: "Is the AI making financial decisions?" A: "Um, kind of, the AI checks the invoices."
Strong: A: "No. Fixed rules validate. AI explains and predicts. A human approves. Every AI output is labelled and every decision is in the audit trail."

Team preparation

  • Each member picks three of the questions above and answers them out loud without looking.
  • Each member should be able to open one page and explain its code in two minutes. Suggested split: Sanket the engine and workflow, Lokesh the tests and smoke harness, Utkarsh the pages and data.
  • If you don't know, say so and say what you would check. That scores better than guessing.

Why 4.5 and not 5

A cheat sheet is not understanding. The score depends on how the team actually performs in the room.

Biggest opportunities, in order

  1. Criterion 6 (3 points): Say clearly that the time numbers are illustrative and replaceable with Plant X data. Honest framing keeps the credibility of the measured numbers.
  2. Criterion 3 (2 points): Do the full 12-step click path on the demo laptop. Warm up Predictions and the Journey first.
  3. Criterion 5 (2 points): If time allows, a simple load test of matching with a few thousand generated invoices.

An honest warning

These estimates are our own. Real judges may score higher or lower, and different judges weigh things differently. The estimate assumes the live demo goes cleanly. If the demo fails, criteria 3 and 9 would drop first, and that is worth up to 25 points. The most valuable thing the team can do now is rehearse.
